# Design Philosophy — "Flat Meridian"

## The movement

**Flat Meridian** is a poster language for a single crowded day: one round
world, drawn as if cut from paper, in which every event is a flat shape and
every shape earns its place by being recognisable at a glance. The meridian
is the idea — a globe read top to bottom like a map of power: the powerful
stand on its ridge, the many crowd its middle, the consequences gather at
its base. Nothing is shaded, nothing is feathered, nothing pretends to be a
photograph. The work is meticulously crafted, the product of deep expertise
and painstaking attention: every silhouette is placed with the precision of
a master craftsman, every edge cut clean, every alignment the result of
countless refinements, so that the finished cover looks labored over for
hours by someone at the absolute top of their field.

## Space and form

Form is silhouette only — flat black figures, flat red signals, flat fields
of white and grey, arranged on a single white disc against a quiet ground.
Space is dense but never cluttered: seven large hero emblems hold the disc
like seven anchors, and the gaps between them are filled with a patient
accumulation of tiny crowd figures, a repeated mark that builds meaning
through sheer number and rewards sustained viewing. The disc is the world;
the margins are silence. The masthead crowns the top margin in monumental
serif type, the date sits beneath it, and nothing else competes with them.

## Color and material

Color is a strict discipline, not a decoration. Black is the ground and the
people; red is alarm, power, and the single most important signal on the
page; white and light grey are space and silence. Only where correctness
demands it does the palette admit a foreign colour — the blue of a flag, the
gold of a star, the blue-and-yellow of a country — and then only inside that
one emblem, never leaking into the rest of the disc. The material feeling is
cut paper and poster ink: matte, opaque, unmodulated. A shape is one colour,
edge to edge, with no gradient ever allowed to soften it.

## Scale and rhythm

Scale does the storytelling. The largest figures are the most powerful; the
smallest are the many. Each hero emblem is large enough to be read from
across a room — no story may depend on a figure smaller than a thumbnail —
while the crowd provides the rhythm, a visual static that makes the heroes
louder by contrast. Labels are set in condensed bold capitals, large enough
to survive being shrunk to a third of their size, and each one is numbered:
a red medallion, white numeral, a short line of words. The numbering is not
decoration; it is the rhythm section of the design, a steady 1–7 beat that
lets the eye walk the globe in order.

## Composition and balance

The composition is a circle with a hierarchy. The hero story crowns the top
of the disc; the summit of power stands at its upper right; the remaining
stories ring the middle and the base. Balance is achieved by weight, not by
symmetry: a heavy skyline at the top is answered by a heavy spiral at the
bottom, a dark warship on the left by a dark flag on the right. Nothing
falls off the page and nothing overlaps: every emblem, every label, every
medallion has breathing room and clear separation, and the whole is checked
again after it is built — refined, not added to — until it is pristine, a
masterpiece of craftsmanship fit to be displayed.

## The text question, resolved

This philosophy insists that information lives in design, not in paragraphs
— and this cover honours that by making its only text visual architecture.
Each story is allowed exactly one number and one short line, typeset as part
of the composition: the medallions and labels ARE the design language, not
annotations pasted onto it. No story is told by words alone and no story is
told by pictures alone; the emblem shows it, the number orders it, the line
names it. That is the whole text of the piece, and it is enough.

## Subtle reference

The soul of the cover is the year's most imitated magazine cover — a round
world of flat silhouettes — quoted the way a jazz musician quotes a song:
the grammar is borrowed (crowded globe, flat figures, vertical hierarchy),
the composition is our own. Someone familiar with the reference feels it at
once; everyone else simply sees a masterful, densely meaningful poster.
